## Problem and outcome

**Where it fits.** ORC-012 added a Shaping stage. The lead asks questions, tracks nine areas of the vision, keeps a draft and proposes a roadmap, and nothing runs until **Start building**. Then the engine runs: many Claude and Codex agents at once, on Autopilot by default, through flows that design, build, check, review, repair and deliver (ORC-021 to ORC-028). This milestone changes what happens before the engine starts, and how the start feels.

**What is wrong today:**

1. **Shaping is all text.** The vision is prose, the coverage is a checklist, and the lead's questions are text. The owner reviews working prototypes, not documents (they said so on 2026-10-01), but sees no screen, flow or command until the factory has built it.
2. **The inputs and outputs are never pinned down.** The forms, requests, files, commands, exit codes and messages are left to each task's agents. They decide them one task at a time, so they can disagree.
3. **Starting the engine feels like a toggle.** "Shaping" and "Building" sound like modes, and **Start building** like a casual button. Nothing says that after it, a powerful engine builds exactly what the vision says, with many agents at once, and that a mistake in the vision is repeated in every task.
4. **Orchestrator's own history shows the cost.** Its first milestone was a UI prototype. About twenty backend milestones then each bolted on their own panel, until an audit found about 300 controls on 13 screens and the whole UI had to be rebuilt (ORC-025). The owner: "I would have like to have started with building the UI out first".

**The outcome:**

- **Two stages, named for what they are.** *Vision*, the studio where nothing is built, and *Factory*, the engine that builds. The words, the screens and the moment of starting all teach that the vision is the step to get right.
- **In the Vision stage, the lead runs a studio loop.** Each round:
  - a designer agent makes artifacts the owner can open, click and mark: demo sites, mockups for browser and mobile, terminal demos and TUIs for CLIs, input and output contracts, and flow maps;
  - the owner marks each one keep, change or drop, pins comments, and picks between variants;
  - the lead revises.
- **The order of the rounds:** the experience first (screens and interaction), then the inputs and outputs, then the flows (the user's journeys and what the system does, including the empty, error and loading states).
- **PE review** (r3, budget r4): a rigid principal engineer (the PE) judges every option on feasibility, scale, longevity and budget.
  - The designer revises until the PE agrees.
  - The PE can ask for probes, small Vision tasks that bring evidence, before it agrees.
  - On Autopilot, the PE also makes the factory's trade-off calls.
- **The approved result is the blueprint:** the vision text plus every approved artifact, versioned. The factory builds from it:
  - task specs cite blueprint items;
  - acceptance comes from its flows and contracts;
  - the UX review checks built work against the approved prototypes.
- **Vision is always a distinct, interactive step, and only the owner starts the factory** (r2, the owner's decision):
  - Every project begins in Vision.
  - A project that already has designs, documents or prototypes still begins there, with them loaded as the first round to review.
  - The factory starts only on the owner's explicit **Start the factory**, from a pre-flight screen that shows:
    - what the blueprint covers and what is still open;
    - what the factory will do: the tasks, the flows, the agents, the autonomy, the delivery and the limits.
  - The start is recorded as the owner's agreement: the blueprint revision and the pre-flight they saw.
  - **Nothing else starts the factory.** Not the lead, not a setting, not Autopilot, and not a timeout. The lead may say the blueprint looks ready and why.
  - Open items are named and confirmed, not blocked on: the owner may start with some open, knowingly.
  - **This gate is the owner's, at the boundary between the stages.** It is not the approval gate on task specs that AGENTS.md rules out. Inside the factory, tasks still run on Autopilot without approval, as today.
- **After the start, a blueprint change is a change order.** A new blueprint revision lists the tasks it affects, and the lead reconciles them through steering (ORC-009).

## Scope

### 1. The factory concept, in words and screens

Following ORC-025's rule of one word per idea:

| The stage where agents build | **Factory** | Building |
| The approved set the factory builds from | **Blueprint** | (new) |
| Starting the engine | **Start the factory** | Start building |
| Going back to the studio | **Back to vision**: the factory finishes what is running and starts nothing new | Back to shaping |
| A blueprint change after the start | **Change order** | (new) |
| The principal engineer's check of each option (r3) | **PE review**; the role is **the PE** | (new) |
| A small Vision task that brings evidence for PE review (r3) | **Probe** | (new) |
| The whole engine paused | **Factory paused** | Paused (project) |

- **The header** shows the stage: *Vision · round 3*, *Factory running · 4 agents*, or *Factory paused*.
- **Home:**
  - In Vision, Home is the studio.
  - In Factory, Home is the factory floor. Progress by area (today's areas) reads as production lines, and Needs you stays first.
- **Two distinct steps, everywhere** (r2):
  - A two-step indicator, *1 Vision* → *2 Factory*, sits in the header on every screen.
  - Vision has its own look as the studio.
  - The factory screens (Tasks, Results) show what is planned during Vision, with nothing running, and say so.
- **No first-run choice to skip Vision** (r2). ORC-012's "Start building now" is removed. The way in for a project with settled designs is **Start from what you have**: the files load as round 0, the lead reviews them, asks what is missing, and the owner marks them and starts the factory.
- **One sentence of framing, said once in the studio:** "The factory builds exactly what the blueprint shows, with many agents at once. Changing the blueprint now takes minutes; changing built work takes runs."
- **Code identifiers keep their names** (`stage: "shaping" | "building"`), as in ORC-025. Only what a person reads changes.
- **The product name** is an open decision for the owner (see Options).

### 2. The studio loop

- **A round** is one exchange: the lead's message, the round's artifacts, and its questions (ORC-012's questions, coverage and draft stay). The owner answers with marks, comments and picks, sent together as one message, like ORC-012's "Send answers".
- **The order of focus,** which the lead may revisit:
  1. **Experience:** the key screens or commands and how they behave.
  2. **Inputs and outputs:** what crosses each boundary, with examples:
     - forms and fields;
     - requests and responses;
     - files;
     - command arguments, standard output, standard error and exit codes;
     - notifications.
  3. **Flows:** the user's journeys, the system's sequence, and the states (empty, loading, error, offline, first run).
- **Who does what:**
  - The **lead** runs the conversation and decides what each round explores.
  - A **designer agent** makes the artifacts in a prototype workspace, with the `experience-first` and `exhaust-the-design-space` principles.
  - A **UX reviewer** may check a round before the owner sees it. This is optional and off by default.
  - **The PE** runs PE review on every round (r3; see below).
  - **Providers (r3):** the owner allows one provider to lead Vision. The lead's proposal:
    - Claude leads and designs, as the owner expects to use Claude most at first.
    - The PE runs on Codex, so the check is independent.
    - The research found that neither provider's artifact tools decide this (section 7).
    - The factory still lets either provider lead, as AGENTS.md requires.
- **Variants:** for an open choice, the designer makes two or three variants side by side, and the owner picks one or mixes them.
- **Feedback:**
  - each artifact is marked **Keep**, **Change** or **Drop**;
  - comments are pinned to a point on a screen or a frame of a terminal demo;
  - free text can be added.

  This is the review the owner used on the UI audit page on 2026-10-01, built into the product.
- **Versions:** every artifact is versioned. A revision shows beside the one before it, with the owner's comments carried over.
- **Approval:**
  - The owner approves artifacts into the blueprint one by one, or a whole round.
  - The lead never approves (r2): this is the one stage designed for the owner's attention.
- **Starting from what you have** (r2): round 0 can be pre-defined artifacts. Each one becomes a studio artifact the owner marks like any other:
  - vision documents (ORC-014);
  - images and mockup files;
  - Penpot or Figma exports;
  - an HTML prototype;
  - screenshots of a running app;
  - a CLI's current help and output.

  The lead summarises what they cover against the experience, inputs and outputs, and flows, and proposes the next round for the gaps.

### 2b. PE review (r3)

- **The PE** is a rigid principal engineer who cares about longevity, scalability, feasibility and budget. It judges each option the designer makes on four things:
  - can it be built with the inputs and technology available?
  - does it hold at the scale the vision states?
  - will it still work and be maintainable in years? (dependencies, data sources, formats)
  - **does it fit the budget?** (r4, "PE also is critical of budget"; see Budget below)
- **Its verdict, per option,** is one of three, with reasons:
  - feasible;
  - feasible if a stated change is made;
  - not feasible, with the evidence that would change the verdict.
- **The loop:** the designer revises until the PE agrees every option it shows the owner is feasible, up to three rounds.
  - If they still disagree, the options go to the owner with the PE's open objections shown. A finding is never dropped (the lesson of ORC-028).
  - The owner may overrule an objection, and the overrule is recorded.
- **Probes:** before it agrees, the PE can ask the lead for evidence. A probe is a small Vision task, for example:
  - a data-availability survey. The owner's example is Council Record: how many city councils publish transcripts, video, agendas and minutes, in what formats, through which platforms, and under what terms;
  - a technical spike: extract structured data from one council's real minutes;
  - a cost or scale estimate;
  - a throwaway prototype of a risky interaction.

  Probes run in a sandboxed workspace, never in the product's repository or the factory, within a Vision budget the owner sets. A probe's result is evidence in the round and in the blueprint.
- **On Autopilot, the PE makes the factory's trade-off calls.** The owner, r3: "If in auto-pilot and trade-offs need to be made those are made by the critic PE".
  - This covers review findings that ask for a decision, and choices between options.
  - On Autopilot they go to the PE instead of to Needs you. ORC-013's routing (`askUserBy`: the owner or the lead) gains the PE, which becomes the default on Autopilot.
  - Each call is recorded with its reasons, shown in the factory, and reversible by the owner.
  - On Check-in and Manual, the calls stay the owner's.
- **Budget (r4):**
  - **The owner sets the budgets in Vision.** The lead asks for them if they are missing:
    - **building:** the agent usage the factory may spend, in dollars for API keys, or as a share of plan usage for subscriptions;
    - **running, per month:** hosting, APIs, data and licences;
    - **probes:** what Vision may spend on evidence.
  - **The PE estimates for each option and for the whole factory,** and objects when an option will not fit.
    - Every estimate states its basis and a range: recorded costs of past runs (`docs/real-runs/` and the project's own attempts), the providers' price lists, or a probe.
    - With no basis, it says so instead of guessing. No claims without evidence.
  - **The factory tracks recorded spend against the building budget.** On reaching it, the factory starts nothing new and asks the owner (Needs you). Usage that a subscription reports only as an estimate is labelled so.
  - **On Autopilot, the PE decides trade-offs within the budget.** A call that would exceed a budget is never the PE's: spending money is a boundary, so it goes to Needs you even on Autopilot.
  - **The pre-flight** shows each budget beside the PE's estimate.
- **Vision itself is unchanged:** there the owner picks between options and starts the factory. The PE advises and checks.
- **The pre-flight** shows whether PE review agreed on every approved item, and which objections the owner overruled.

### 3. What the artifacts are (by product type)

| Product type | Experience | Inputs and outputs | Flows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Web app | A clickable demo site at desktop, tablet and phone sizes | Form fields, API requests and responses, with examples | Journey maps and sequence diagrams, plus state screens |
| Mobile app | Screens in a device frame, clickable; mockups for platform-specific views | The same, plus permissions and notifications | The same, plus offline and background states |
| CLI | Terminal demos of each command: typed input, output, colour, errors; a TUI mockup where there is one | A command reference: arguments, flags, standard input and output, exit codes, config files | The command sequences of each journey, with failure paths |
| API or library | Example calls and responses, a quickstart | A schema with examples | Sequence diagrams |

- **The lead proposes the type from the vision and the repository,** and the owner confirms it. A product can have more than one, such as an app with a CLI.
- **The tools that make and show each kind** are in section 7. They must be open-source or local-first, with pinned versions, as the owner prefers mature open-source tools to custom ones.

### 4. The blueprint and the factory

- **Storage:** the blueprint is versioned like the vision. It holds:
  - the vision text;
  - the approved artifacts, each with an id, its kind and its files;
  - the contracts;
  - the flows.
- **What it changes in the factory:**
  - The lead's task specs cite the blueprint items they build.
  - The acceptance scenarios come from the flows and the contract examples.
  - The Feature flow's design step starts from the approved prototype instead of from nothing.
  - The UX review checks the built screens against the approved prototype screens.
- **Change orders:** a blueprint revision after the factory has started lists the affected tasks (those that cite the changed items). The lead proposes the changes through steering, with Undo.

### 5. The pre-flight screen

Shown by **Start the factory**. It reads in one screen:

- **The blueprint:**
  - approved screens and commands;
  - contracts;
  - flows;
  - ORC-012's nine areas;
  - anything still open, by name.
- **What the factory will do:**
  - the planned tasks and their flows;
  - the providers and models by role;
  - Autopilot, Check-in or Manual;
  - delivery (branch or pull request, and who merges);
  - agents at once, and the run limits.
- **Budget (r4):** the owner's building and running budgets, beside the PE's estimates, each with its basis and range; and where the factory will stop and ask.
- **The decision:** **Start the factory**, the owner's action only.
  - Open items are listed and confirmed, and an empty vision blocks, as today.
  - The start records the owner's agreement: who, when, the blueprint revision, and the pre-flight summary.
  - A start through ORC-023's Claude Code plugin needs the owner's explicit word and shows the same pre-flight first.
- **Existing projects** already in Building become Factory in the migration. **Back to vision** stays available: running work finishes, and nothing new starts until the owner starts the factory again.

### 6. Safety

- **Prototypes are code an agent wrote.** The service serves each one from its own workspace:
  - on a separate origin, inside a sandboxed iframe (`allow-scripts`, no same-origin);
  - with a content security policy that allows no network;
  - with no access to Orchestrator's API.
- **Terminal demos must not run anything real.** A tool that records terminals runs a shell. For a CLI that does not exist yet, the designer writes a script that prints the planned output, and the recording runs in the agent sandbox with the network off.
- **Nothing is published outside the machine without the owner's approval of that publish** (r3, from the sharing research in section 7):
  - **Share** publishes one copy, private by default, after the owner approves it. The approval shows the provider, the audience, the files and the version. The record keeps the URL, the audience, the version and the time.
  - **The studio's local copy stays the source of truth.** Neither provider's published pages can be framed inside the canvas: both send `frame-ancestors 'self'`. So shared copies open in a new tab.
  - **Native publishing is off for studio agents** (the designer, the PE and probes). Claude's Artifact tool and Codex's Sites plugin can both publish without a confirmation of their own.
    - Claude workers already run with an explicit tool list that leaves it out.
    - Isolated Codex runs already disable plugins and apps.
  - **Codex's "Use my local setup" mode** can reach the owner's Sites plugin. Settings must say so, and studio agents never run in that mode.

**Patterns borrowed, not depended on:**

- Click an element to comment on it or change it, from v0's Design Mode, Lovable's preview toolbar and Figma Make.
- Inline comments and a hand-off bundle to the coding agent, from Claude Design (Anthropic Labs, April 2026).
- Each prototype on its own origin with a strict content security policy, as Claude's artifacts are.

**Safety details from the research:**

- `sandbox="allow-scripts"` without `allow-same-origin`: together they cancel the sandbox.
- A separate hostname per prototype (such as `p-<id>.localhost`), because cookies are shared across ports.
- `default-src 'self'; connect-src 'none'; form-action 'none'`, with every asset served locally.
- Prototypes use local fixtures for data.

3. VHS and Chromium inside the sandbox without network.
4. Whether textual-serve loads assets from a CDN.
5. Whether ink-web is mature enough.
6. Whether `*.localhost` resolves the same way in the owner's browsers.
7. Whether Figma's pricing after its beta is acceptable, if the owner wants it.

### 8. The demo and the tests

- **The demo** gains a Vision story, with pre-rendered artifacts labelled simulated: a round, marks, a variant picked, the blueprint, the pre-flight and the start.
- **The real-run scenario** gains one studio round with a real designer agent. It checks that a prototype exists, is served sandboxed, and renders.
- (r2) **The scenario starts the factory the way the owner does:** Start the factory from the pre-flight, with the agreement recorded. Today the scenario creates projects directly in Building.

**Excluded:**

- Hosting prototypes publicly.
- Two-way sync with Figma or Penpot. It may come later, depending on the research and the owner's accounts.
- Usability testing with real users.
- A studio per task. Tasks already have the Design flow.

## Options and tradeoffs

**The approach:**

| Option | Approach | User benefit | Effort | Costs and risks | Reversibility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A (recommended)** | The studio lives in Orchestrator: a designer agent writes prototypes, terminal demos, contracts and flow maps into a prototype workspace; the app shows them and collects marks | Everything in one place, local, and reviewable as the owner likes; the blueprint links straight into the factory | Large: 6 passes | Agent-made prototypes vary in quality; serving agent code needs the sandboxing above | High: a stage and new artifact kinds |
| B | External design tools are the canvas (Figma or Penpot through MCP); the app links out | A real design tool | Medium | Accounts and plans; agent write access is uncertain; not local-first; the blueprint lives elsewhere | Medium |
| C | Keep text shaping, and let the lead attach screenshots | Small | Small | Not what the owner asked: no clicking, no iteration on real artifacts | High |

**The product name** (decided by the owner in r2: keep "Orchestrator"):

| Option | What changes | For | Against |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Keep "Orchestrator" (recommended)** | Only the two stages are renamed: Vision and Factory | The name describes the whole tool: one lead orchestrating many agents. The repository, README and links stay | The factory metaphor lives inside the product, not in its name |
| Rename to "Factory" | The product, the README, the repository name and links | The metaphor is the first thing people see | A rename across the repository and links, as with ORC-008's rename; "factory" alone loses the lead and the vision |
| Both, as a tagline | "Orchestrator: a vision studio and an agent factory" | Explains the two stages at a glance | Longer |

## Decision

- Agent recommendation:
  - Approach A.
  - Keep the product name, adding the tagline if the owner likes it.
  - The owner approves the blueprint by default.
  - Build in the passes below, experience first.
- Selected option: **A**, keeping the name "Orchestrator" (the owner, r2).
  - Vision is a distinct, always interactive step.
  - Only the owner's explicit Start starts the factory, also when Vision begins from pre-defined artifacts (the owner, r2).
- Decided by: the owner (the name, Vision as a distinct step, the start), and the lead (the approach and the passes).
- Rationale and evidence:
  - The owner reviews prototypes, not text.
  - Orchestrator's own UI had to be rebuilt for lack of a design first.
  - The ORC-027 and ORC-028 runs showed that agents follow a written spec loosely, and a reviewable artifact plus a review loop is what kept them on it.
- Uncertainty and what would change the decision:
  - How good agent-made prototypes are, and how fast. Pass 1 builds the studio on simulated artifacts, and pass 3 tries real ones before the rest is built.
  - Penpot's MCP is the one route to true design files. If it cannot run without the owner watching, mockups stay HTML and Excalidraw, and Penpot or Figma becomes an export (part of option B).
